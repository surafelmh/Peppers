The Science Behind the Spice

The source of spice in chili peppers is capsaicin. Capsaicin functions by binding to specific receptors in your mouth and on your tongue that detect physical heat and pain. It interacts with these receptors by lowering the threshold they require to trigger sensations similar to physically touching something that is burning hot. This causes no damage to your mouth whatsoever, it is purely a chemical trick.

The Natural Purpose of Capsaicin

The capsaicin found in chili peppers serves a multitude of purposes in nature. One of the many benefits is by providing chilies protection from fungi. Many fungi are pathogenic to chilies plants, but through the antifungal properties of capsaicin, the plant does not have to worry about fungal infections. Furthermore capsaicin provides protection from insects. Insects feeding on specific areas of the chilies can result in damage and can leave them vulnerable to damage from other sources as well. However, capsaicin's toxicity to insects leaves the insects searching for a meal elsewhere. One of the more major purposes of capsaicin is by preventing mammals from eating it because they can damage the plant's seeds. Although mammals feel the spice, birds do not, and are able to spread their seeds to promote the plant's growth.

Why Spice can Vary

The amount of capsaicin in a pepper depends on outside influences. Heat, drought, irregular watering, and other stresses can result in a plant producing more capsaicin. Another factor is sun exposure, which can also result in more capsaicin. All of these differences may seem small, but the difference in spice can have huge results. Furthermore, knowledge of these factors allows farmers to create peppers with their desired spice level. The possibility of outliers with completely random spice levels still exists, but it is not very common.
